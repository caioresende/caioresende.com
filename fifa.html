UX Case Study

FIFA 20 Global Series

Two Battlefy projects built on one idea: ask for as little as possible, at the moment it matters. I redesigned EA's FIFA 20 Global Series fan hub so it reorders itself around what's live and one team pick. On Battlefy's tournament platform, a one-tap rating flow took the share of players rating a tournament from 0.5% to 16% over four A/B-tested rounds.

Product Designer at Battlefy · 2019–2020 Esports · Responsive Web · Fans & Players Adobe XD · Miro · Qualtrics

01

Every visitor got the same static page

The FIFA 20 Global Series was EA's competitive program for FIFA: pro and amateur players competing in regional tournaments, with prize pools and live broadcasts for a global audience of millions. Battlefy ran the competitive infrastructure behind it. I was the product designer on the fan-facing hub, from the experience audit through IA, wireframes and final design, working with Battlefy's product and engineering teams.

The hub served two audiences with different needs. Players needed to find tournaments, register, and follow schedules and results in their region. Viewers wanted the live stream, match stats and highlights. Data showed the site wasn't working for either, and the brief came down to one question: how might we increase engagement, with viewership and rewards in mind?

What the audit found

The navigation weighted the wrong things

Official rules and About had the same weight as everything else, while sign-up and log-in were easy to miss. The reward for watching, and the reason to create an account, never landed.

The best spot on the page was generic

The most valuable space on the page showed generic content, not the match that was live or the visitor's own team.

Events weren't relevant to most people

Event tiles used look-alike logos and weren't filtered by region or team, so most visitors were looking at events that had nothing to do with them.

Carousels hid the content

Content sat behind carousel slides, out of sight until someone clicked through.

The shift

Two findings pointed at the same gap: the page didn't know what was live or who was looking. I organized the redesign around those two inputs, what's live and which team you follow, and let them decide what each visitor sees first.

02

Live comes first, then follows you down the page

The biggest structural decision was to treat video and broadcast as one module with three states, instead of designing three different pages. The page reorders itself around what's happening.

  • Default: the top of the page leads with videos and news for your team.
  • Match live: the stream and score take the top. Match facts like shots, possession and pass accuracy open next to the stream when you want them.
  • Browsing during a match: the stream moves into a floating picture-in-picture player, so you keep watching while you read news or check events.

Because these are states of one module, they carry over to a phone, stacked into a single column.

Wireframes for default, live and picture-in-picture. The black block is the same media module in every frame: a slim bar by default, the full-width hero during a match, and a floating window while you browse.

Two smaller pieces support the live experience. A score strip under the navigation shows what's live and starting soon, designed to put your team and your region first. Below the media, a "What's happening" grid replaced the old carousels: events, news and videos in one grid you can filter, with a "Load more" button.

Final design: default, live, live with match facts, and picture-in-picture.

03

Your team, and a reason to sign in

Region comes from the account, so the only thing the hub asks for is a favorite team. The picker says what you get in return: exclusive content from your team. After that one choice, videos, news, events and live scores are set up to lead with your team.

Log-in lives in EA's global header, shared across every EA product, so the hub had to give people a reason to use it. My Coins rewards watching, video modules carry a call to action to earn coins, and the balance sits in the navigation next to region and team.

Navigation in two layers

EA's global header stayed consistent with the rest of EA. I redesigned the hub's own bar underneath it: Watch, Events, Rewards, Standings and News are the primary paths, the rules and About moved out of the top level, and region, team and coins sit together on the right.

On mobile the menu collapses, and those three personal controls get their own row at the top of the open menu, so they're never buried under the links.

The hub's final desktop bar: the primary paths on the left, and region, team and coins together on the right.

The team picker, and the open mobile menu with the personal controls in their own row.

04

From 0.5% of players rating tournaments to 16%

On the competitive side, I worked on player ratings in Battlefy's tournament platform. Players could already rate a tournament, but only 0.5% of the players who took part ever did, which left organizers with very little feedback to act on. The initial goal was to grow that by 3%.

I grounded the redesign in user research and in how Uber asks for ratings: ask at the right moment, make the first answer one tap, and offer structured options for both good and bad experiences.

The flow

One question, one tap

Thumbs up or down. The copy matches the moment: "Enjoying this tournament?" while it's running, "Did you enjoy this tournament?" once it's over.

Structured follow-up

A thumbs up offers compliments like well organized, clear rules and great prizing. A thumbs down asks what could be better: wait times, admin management, format, skill balance or bugs.

A next step

Both paths end with a thank-you and somewhere to go: follow the organizer or find another tournament.

One prompt, four places

I mapped user flows for every place the rating prompt could appear: as a floating card after log-in, on the tournament page, on the match page, and after the tournament ended. Because the question follows the tournament's state, the same prompt fits all four.

Four rounds of A/B tests

Across four iterations we A/B tested triggers, copy and follow-up options, and each round beat the previous target. Player satisfaction went from 62% to 80%, and by the last round, 16% of players were leaving a rating.

Closing the loop for organizers

Ratings fed an organizer dashboard: player count, new followers, approval rating over time, and a breakdown of compliments and areas to improve. Organizers got specific reasons to improve their events, drawn from their own players.

The common thread

Both pieces of work came down to the same move: ask for the smallest possible input, at the moment it's easiest to give. One team pick personalizes the hub. One tap starts a rating, and organizers get specific feedback in return.
