UX Case Study · Comentto

Rebuilding a Kiosk Feedback Platform

Comentto puts survey tablets on kiosks and sends each answer to managers as it's given. As the only designer, I audited the kiosk app, built a design system, and redesigned device setup, the survey builder, the kiosk and the reports on top of it, around one positive, neutral and negative scale that managers set when they write a question and read at the top of every survey report.

UX/UI Designer Consultant · Nov 2017 – Oct 2018 Real-time customer feedback Kiosk app · Web admin · Mobile wireframes

01

One Survey, Three Audiences

Comentto collects feedback where it happens. Tablets on kiosks stand where customers, patients, employees or visitors pass by, and each answer reaches the managers' dashboard as soon as it's given. Clients ranged across aerospace, logistics, food production, insurance and education. The screens on this page use restaurant and cafeteria surveys as examples.

Every survey has three audiences:

  • The respondent stands at a kiosk, on a device that isn't theirs, with a few seconds to spare. No one is there to explain anything.
  • The manager who sets it up writes and branches surveys, pairs kiosks and assigns them to locations in a web admin panel.
  • The manager who reads the results does it on the dashboard at a desk, or on a phone while walking the floor.

Each audience needed its own interface. What they had to share was one definition of a good, neutral or bad answer, and that became the thread through the whole project.

The three surfaces: the kiosk a respondent meets, the admin panel with its live response feed, and the general report a manager reads.

My role

I was the only designer, working as an outside consultant with an engineering lead, a business requirements analyst and the business director. I owned the audit, the design system, the interaction patterns and the visual design across web and mobile.

Phase 1 took two months: the usability audit, the design system outline and the core screens. Then two one-month phases ran side by side: the survey builder and the survey respondents take, and the dashboards, reports and mobile analytics app.

02

What the Audit Found on the Kiosk

Phase 1 started with a usability analysis of the existing product. The web panel's problem was a familiar one: it ran on stock Bootstrap, every new screen brought its own layout, and nothing connected it to the kiosk. The kiosk app's problems cost more, because nobody standing at a kiosk gets training or a second try.

I annotated the kiosk app screen by screen. Each finding ends with what the redesign changed.

No way out, and no sign of life

The idle screen had no way out, so the only exit was force-quitting the app. And after a manager configured the device in the web panel, the app didn't update or give any feedback. Redesign: the waiting screen says what to do next, shows that it's waiting for a connection, and has Exit and Refresh.

Progress you had to decode, buttons that moved

A bare progress bar left people to guess how far along they were, and Back and Next moved with the length of each question, often out of thumb reach. Redesign: every screen says "4 of 10" under the bar, and Back and Next stay pinned to the bottom.

One answer or several? No way to tell

Single- and multiple-choice options looked the same, and both looked like text fields. Redesign: radio buttons for one answer, checkboxes for several, and Next stays disabled until something is checked.

Icons that nudge the score

Hearts and thumbs-up on a plain rating suggest a feeling the question never asked about, which can sway the score. Redesign: the survey builder groups icons by whether they express satisfaction (more below).

Long number scales broke the layout

More than five buttons wrapped onto a second row on smaller screens. Redesign: 1–10 questions use a star dial, or a slider with labeled ends.

Hard to read, slow to type

Light type was hard to read in a hurry, open-text answers had no limit, and entering a birth date meant paging back through a calendar. Redesign: heavier type with a text-size setting per survey, a 200-character counter, and dates you can type or pick.

The annotated analysis of the original kiosk app (in Portuguese), including a thumb-reach map for touch screens.

The approach

The audit became a prioritized backlog. Instead of redesigning everything at once, we sequenced it: the design system first, then each surface rebuilt on top of it.

03

One Component Set, Specced for Every State

I replaced Bootstrap with a custom system built the atomic way: tokens for color, type and spacing, then controls, then panels, then page templates. I specced every component for engineering with its states and spacing, so that a new screen could be assembled from existing parts instead of invented.

Sentiment as a system rule

Positive, neutral and negative get green, yellow and red, the traffic light people already know. The summary indicator pairs each color with a face so the value still reads without color. The same split runs from the options a manager tags in the builder to the reports.

The sentiment indicator spec. Each value pairs a color with a face, and the same component opens each survey report.

States and recovery

Buttons, checkboxes, radio buttons and toggles are specced active, pressed and disabled; text inputs add placeholder, required and error states. Adding a device ends in a toast with Undo, so a mistake takes one tap to reverse.

One pattern, many screens

The survey builder's question-type picker and the dashboard builder's widget picker share one pattern, so building a dashboard works like building a survey. The charts in "Investigate question" sit in one card that switches between line and bar, goes full screen, or shows its numbers as a table. One page template with a collapsible sidebar frames every admin screen.

Once the system shipped, engineering could build new pages from existing parts without a design review on every screen.

Controls and feedback: buttons, inputs and selection controls in each state, and the toast pattern: success with Undo, removal, and a plain message.

Shared patterns: the question-type and widget pickers, the chart card with its table view, and the page template with its collapsible sidebar.

04

Sentiment Is Set When the Question Is Written

The old builder was one long form with no preview. I rebuilt it around the shape of the survey itself: welcome, questions and thank-you, the same three stages a respondent sees. Managers add question types from a panel and drag them into order, and four tabs split the job: structure, visual, logic jumps, and settings and sharing. Every question type follows the same layout, so learning one teaches the rest.

Choices carry their sentiment

When a manager writes a multiple-choice question, they tag each option positive, neutral or negative. Rating icons come in two groups. Scales that run from negative to positive, like sad-to-happy faces or thumbs up and down, reflect satisfaction. Icons that only count, like stars, a smiley, a heart or a single thumbs-up, don't. The audit recommended stars alone for plain ratings. The builder keeps the other icons but files them under "doesn't reflect satisfaction", so the manager chooses knowingly.

Because choices and satisfaction scales carry a sentiment from the moment they're written, every survey report can open with the share of positive, neutral and negative answers, whatever else the survey asked. Long-text answers, which carry no sentiment, get a word-cloud widget on the dashboard instead.

Branching, theming and sharing

Logic jumps read as a sentence (if this question's answer is this, go to that question), with And/Or for compound rules, so managers branch a survey without programming. The Visual tab sets the survey's colors and a text size (Normal, Large or Huge) with a live preview. Settings limit a survey to chosen units, cap responses per device (no limit, one, or one a day), and generate a link and QR code per unit for people who aren't at a kiosk.

The builder: the three-stage structure, sentiment-tagged options, rating icons grouped by meaning, sentence-style logic, the Visual tab and sharing settings.

05

The Kiosk, from Setup to Answering

Setting up a kiosk

Pairing spans two surfaces, so I designed it as one flow. A freshly installed kiosk opens on a waiting screen that tells staff the one thing to do next: load a survey from the admin panel. In the admin, staff pick the device's unit and survey, then pair it by scanning the QR code shown there with the Comentto app or by typing the 9-digit code. A help popover walks through installing the app and both ways to pair.

The kiosk then appears in the device table with its survey, unit and online status. Managers can update or remove several kiosks at once, and every add or remove can be undone from the toast. On a tablet, the side panel becomes a modal.

Pairing and managing kiosks: the waiting screen, adding a device, the pairing help, bulk actions, Undo after removal, and the tablet layout.

Answering standing up

Each screen asks one question. Questions sit left-aligned, where the eye starts reading, and a selected option fills with color so the choice is obvious at arm's length. Each client's colors come from the builder, and the same components render every theme.

A survey in progress: a client-themed welcome, single choice, multi-select with Next disabled until something is checked, open text with a 200-character limit, and a date you can type or pick.

A rating for each kind of question

Stars for plain scores, faces where the question measures satisfaction, and for 1–10, a star dial or a slider labeled from "fell short" to "exceeded expectations".

Rating patterns: a 5-star score, a 1–10 dial, a star slider, a labeled 1–10 slider, and a face scale reserved for satisfaction.

06

Reports That Lead with Sentiment

A manager opening a report wants to know how things are going first, so every survey report opens with the response count and the share of positive, neutral and negative answers, then breaks down each question. "Investigate question" plots one question's sentiment over time. Reports filter by unit and period and can be downloaded.

Investigate question: the summary indicators, then one question's positive, neutral and negative share over time, in chart cards that switch between line and bar, go full screen or show a table.

Each unit (here, a restaurant) has a satisfaction goal and optional shifts. The units list shows each goal beside a panel with current satisfaction by unit and Comentto's satisfaction index for bars and restaurants. For a standing view, managers build their own dashboards from six widgets: chart, summary, table, indicator, unit satisfaction and word cloud.

From report to dashboard: the report summary with per-question charts, a unit's goal and shifts, the units list, the dashboard builder and saved dashboards.

On the phone

Not every manager sits at a desk. For the ones on the floor, I wireframed a companion app: an overview of overall satisfaction, units against their goals, a per-unit breakdown, a period picker and notifications.

Mobile analytics wireframes: overview, units against goals, unit detail, period settings and notifications.

07

What I'd Do Differently

Test at the kiosk, not at my desk

The audit and the redesign came from heuristics and best practices. I never watched people use a kiosk where it was installed. A worker in gloves on a factory floor and a visitor in a lobby need different things, and I could only guess at both.

Don't let color carry the trends

The summary indicators pair each color with a face, and the charts in "Investigate question" have a table view, but the trend lines themselves are told apart only by green, yellow and red. I'd add direct labels or line styles so the charts work for color-blind managers too.

Design the offline kiosk

Kiosks in factories and remote sites lose their connection. I designed the waiting state for a kiosk with no survey, but not what a kiosk shows, or how it holds answers, when it drops offline in the middle of a day.

Measure how the system is used

I documented every component but never tracked which ones engineers used and which they rebuilt, so I couldn't improve the system based on real use.

What I'd keep is the core decision: define what a good answer is once, where the question is written, and let every surface inherit it.
