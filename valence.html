UX Case Study

Valence

40% of teams created on Valence never ran a single exercise, and renewals depended on usage. I redesigned the first mile; within two weeks of launch, activation conversion was up 10%. The work was part of bringing four products under one design language.

Design Lead · Sep 2021 – Mar 2023 Enterprise SaaS · Team development Figma · Heap · FullStory

01

Bought by HR, stalled at the team leader

Valence sold four team-development tools, including Align, a virtual team facilitator, to companies like Coca-Cola, Nestle and Vanguard. HR and L&D teams bought the licenses. Team leaders decided whether their teams ever used them.

Renewals depended on license usage, so a team that never ran an exercise was a renewal at risk. One client, Coca-Cola, was a $400,000 contract. And 40% of teams created on the platform never activated.

Leaders were the multiplier. One leader who didn't start could mean five, ten or twenty inactive members. They were also the ones leaving: after onboarding, leaders bounced at three times the rate of team members.

The goal

Get a team leader from account creation to their team's first exercise.

40% of teams created on the platform never activated. Below, each cohort's time to first activity; red is teams that never activated.

My role

I joined as Valence's first in-house design lead, after several rounds of contractors, with a two-part mandate: one design language across all four products, and a consistent, clean experience in the five months before the Series A. On this project I led two designers and worked with three user interviewers, a customer success rep and a UX writer, across discovery, solution design and testing.

02

Watching leaders instead of asking them

We had eight weeks from discovery to engineering and a small team. An earlier round of client interviews hadn't helped: busy senior leaders gave polished answers we couldn't act on. So I moved the research to behavior.

We wrote down three hypotheses first: leaders don't see why the tools matter, don't know where to start, and can't make sense of what's on offer. Then I mapped the journey from registration to a team's first closed exercise, branch by branch through onboarding, and annotated every step with FullStory sessions and Heap drop-off data. Usability tests on the existing flow filled in the why.

The existing onboarding, mapped branch by branch from role choice to the tab each answer lands on, annotated with bugs, routing logic and UX notes.

30% skipped onboarding

And for those who finished, the answers barely shaped what came next. The questions collected data without meaningfully personalizing the experience.

Managers and HR partners got the same path

They came with different needs, but onboarding sent them through the same steps. There was no way back to a previous step, either.

Clear, but slow to scan

Testers understood the questions but spent too long reading plain lists of options.

Lost on arrival

After onboarding, leaders landed on a page full of options with nothing telling them where to start. In testing they switched tabs right away and didn't see value on first contact.

What it added up to

Leaders left because nothing told them where to start. The gap between “registered” and “activated” was a first-mile design problem.

03

Onboarding that previews a first step

We picked three things to fix: the onboarding questions and UI, the first page after onboarding, and the product pages that explain each tool. The questions and the product pages are below. Where leaders landed after onboarding stayed generic, and I come back to that at the end.

The old questions were plain radio lists and checkboxes, with a “Does not apply” button beside Next. I rebuilt them as large cards with a role label and one line of description (Team member, Manager, HRBP/facilitator), so people could pick an answer at a glance, and dropped “Does not apply”. I also added a step on purpose: four light screens where there had been three dense ones, with the Previous button the old flow lacked.

The last step changed the most. Instead of a checklist of team worries, leaders pick one objective, like Build trust or Uncover blind spots, and see a preview of an exercise that fits it.

Every iteration was an interactive Figma prototype, so testers clicked through role, objective and landing page in order.

First step, before and after: a radio list became role cards. Last step, before and after: a checklist became one objective, with a preview of a matching exercise.

04

Product pages that sell the outcome

A leader who opened Align saw three small cards and a “Start the diagnostic” button. Little about what the team would get out of it, no proof from other leaders, no answers to the obvious doubts. That's a lot to take on faith before putting your whole team through a diagnostic.

I benchmarked how a calendar app, Apple Pages, Officevibe and a Twitter analytics tool introduce themselves to new users. The best ones lead with what the user gets, then prove it.

The benchmarks, then the Align page before and after.

The new page opens with the question a manager actually has, “Want to know what matters most to your team?”, with a sample report beside the Start button and a persistent Start Align bar at the top. Everything below answers one doubt at a time: why managers use Align, Vanguard leaders in their own words, and an FAQ on team size, inviting colleagues, and the risk to the person running the exercise. I built these as a set of new components for the product pages, from the start bar to the testimonial and FAQ cards.

Two iterations of the benefits section: six blocks of copy, and two sections that show the product. Next, the FAQ, Vanguard testimonials and a call-to-action block.

To let leaders see the exercise before putting their team through it, I compared two previews side by side: a real screen capture of Align, and a simplified motion graphic with bigger cards and a single cursor. After launch, people spent more time on product pages with animated previews.

The two previews: a real screen capture of the Align exercise, and the simplified motion graphic.

05

What moved

The goal was getting team leaders to a first exercise. We shipped in phases and tracked it in Heap and FullStory.

+10% Activation conversion

The early read, within two weeks of launch.

8 wks Discovery to engineering

2 weeks of discovery, 4 of iteration and testing, 2 of engineering.

What the data told us next

Most people didn't scroll to the bottom of the product pages, but they did use the calls to action at the top. The first screen has to carry the decision; everything below it is for the people still on the fence.

Where it stopped short

Routing after onboarding stayed generic. We already knew each leader's role and objective, so a manager after team bonding and an HRBP evaluating the platform for several teams should have landed in different places.

06

From one flow to one design language

Back to the first half of my mandate: one design language across all four products.

The new product pages, the redesigned onboarding and the in-product motion previews were all part of a suite-wide redesign under a universal design language and a new design system. That improved email conversion, cross-selling and consistency across products, increased usage, and supported renewals.

Across my time at Valence, weekly iteration with product and engineering lifted onboarding completion 30%. Along the way I grew the design team across multiple countries and partnered with leadership on the rebrand after the Series A.

Perspective, Align, Reflect 360 and Habits: the four tools, each with its own color in one shared visual style.
