Case Study · Battlefy

Portals

Battlefy built every branded tournament site from scratch, like an agency. I turned those one-offs into a product: three tiers assembled from shared components, with rules for the content each client brings. It cut delivery from four weeks to one and became the platform's most important revenue source. The same design system also carried league registration, eligibility and approval flows.

01

Every Portal Was a One-Off

Battlefy is an esports tournament platform. Its B2B side built "Portals" for brands and game publishers such as Riot Games, EA, Nintendo and Red Bull: branded sites where players find a program's tournaments, register and compete.

Every portal was its own project, with custom design, custom code and custom assets. A fix on one portal did nothing for the others. Each one took four weeks to deliver, so the team couldn't take on more clients without hiring, and the cost of delivery was growing faster than revenue.

My Role

I led design on turning Portals into a product, in a small team with a PM, an engineer and sales. I interviewed people from every team involved in delivery, redesigned how engagements ran, defined the three tiers and specified the components in Comet. It was the first project at Battlefy to test whether the design function could scale.

The Bet

Across dozens of portals, the brands looked nothing alike, but they asked for the same things: a hero, a way to start competing, prizes, tournament listings, answers to common questions, social links and streams. If the goals repeated, the work could be a product instead of a project.

02

The Real Problem Was Upstream

Problems kept surfacing in client engagements, but the root cause wasn't clear. Before designing anything, I interviewed people from every team involved in delivery about ownership, blockers, deadlines, how teams worked together, and what each person wanted to start, stop and keep doing.

Engineers

They were building in a rush from designs that weren't final or validated. Comet, Battlefy's design system, could help, but wasn't being used to its potential.

Product managers

They were blocked by clients, had more engagements than capacity, and wanted clear goals before execution started.

Designers

The gap between design and engineering validation slowed everything down. Testing had to happen sooner.

Sales and account managers

Client relationships got strained during and after reviews, and they wanted a more concrete process for QA and client feedback.

What the Interviews Showed

We had been asking how to deliver faster. The answer sat earlier in the process: we weren't understanding client goals early enough. Rework, missed deadlines and frustration on every team traced back to that. So the fix had two parts: bring the product team into client engagements early, then turn what we learned there into patterns every portal could reuse.

Clear Owners, Earlier Involvement

I redesigned how branded engagements ran. The product team now joined at problem definition instead of receiving requirements at handoff, with cross-team checkpoints at data analysis, user flows, wireframes and mockups. The work was split into four lanes around kits, the shared component sets every portal is assembled from:

  • Sales, account managers and solution architects own the client: deadlines, assets, special requests, satisfaction and feedback.
  • Design owns the kits: strategy, requirements, components, documentation and testing with users.
  • Engine engineers build, version and maintain the kits.
  • Frontline engineers turn the kits into each client's portal, and request kit updates when a client needs something new.

Each lane answered something from the interviews: engineers would build from documented kits instead of unvalidated designs, PMs would have client goals before execution started, design could test earlier at each checkpoint, and sales would own client satisfaction and feedback.

That split is what let one set of components serve many clients: built once, assembled many times. To remove the back-and-forth that opened every engagement, I also wrote a guide telling clients exactly which assets to send: style guide, logos, color palette and hero imagery.

03

Three Tiers, One Set of Rules

I audited the portals we had built and compiled the components they used most. The audit confirmed the bet: what varied was the branding and how much engagement a client wanted. So I packaged the product as three tiers, each named for the client goal it serves:

Tier 1 · Player acquisition

Client branding, tournaments, prizing, game listings, social links and FAQ. A branded home that brings players into a competitive program.

Tier 2 · Player diversity

Everything in Tier 1, plus multiple regions and localization for programs that run across countries and languages.

Tier 3 · Player engagement

Tiers 1 and 2, plus loyalty and rewards, leaderboards, social hubs, streaming and hospitality, for brands that want an ongoing relationship with their community.

Rules for the Content, Not Just the Layout

Every component was specified in Comet, the design system I co-built and maintained, for desktop and mobile. Each spec set rules for the content a client would bring: character limits, when text truncates and gets a tooltip, image sizes and formats, which color tokens to use, and what to fall back to when a client doesn't have an asset. With those rules and a client's assets, a portal could be themed and assembled without designing it from scratch.

Comet specs for portal components, desktop and mobile. The rules include: hero titles truncate with a tooltip past 70 characters, onboarding is capped at three steps, awards fall back to a library icon when a client has no prize image, and signing in from the Battlefy bar returns players to where they were.

The Monumental Sports & Entertainment portal, from wireframe to final design. The wireframe shares the hero and card structure of the specs above; the final applies MSE's brand.

04

One System, Many Brands

Each portal was themed from the client's style guide and assets: type, color, imagery and spacing. EA's Madden Championship Series, Riot's college and partner series, Bang & Olufsen and MLB The Show share one skeleton (hero, navigation, prizing, tournaments and help) under the same Battlefy account bar. Each brand got a portal that felt like its own; Battlefy got one product to build and maintain.

Tier features appear only where a program needs them. Bang & Olufsen's portal switches between North America and Europe and has a language picker. MLB The Show lays out two paths, competitive and casual. Riot's Partner Series adds influencer profiles, streams and a live bracket. College League of Legends is a full league site with teams, players and fantasy.

High-fidelity mockups for EA Madden Championship Series, Riot Grand Prix College Club Series and Partner Series, Bang & Olufsen and College League of Legends. Each puts the client's brand on the same Battlefy bar, hero and portal navigation.

05

Making League Rules Clear When They Matter

The biggest programs ran whole seasons, not single events. Rocket League's RLCS X, the Apex Legends Global Series and College League of Legends needed teams, linked game accounts, rule acceptance, eligibility checks and admin approvals. These flows were built from Comet components too, so a flow could be rebranded and its steps reconfigured for another client. They are full of rules, and the design work was making each rule clear at the moment it mattered.

Registration With the Rules in Plain Sight

RLCS X registration had to collect a lot: rule acceptance, a linked platform account, legal name, date of birth and email, team details and teammate invites. The flow splits it into five steps with a progress list that stays visible, and each consequence sits next to the decision it belongs to. Step one asks players to accept the critical rules. Beyond that, the rules with lasting effects sit where they apply: on the link-account step, an "Important rules" box beside the in-game name field says a player can be removed for a name that doesn't represent them, and that neither the name nor the platform can change after the team's first match. "Register as staff" stays disabled until the player confirms that staff can manage a team but can't play.

The first screen asks a single question: create a team or join one? College League of Legends uses the same entry ("Are you a team captain?"), so captains and invited teammates each land on the right path.

RLCS X registration, from choosing a path to joining from an invite, followed by the same entry pattern in College League of Legends.

A Team Is Ready Only When Everyone Is

In the Apex Legends Global Series, a team could only compete once every player in the lineup had registered and accepted the rules. That one rule creates many states: two players missing, one missing, team registered, check-in open, checked in, games in progress, and disqualified when someone didn't register in time. I mapped every state and gave each actionable state a single next action (join, copy the invite link, check in, go to the tournament), with a countdown wherever a deadline applied.

The states also say what happens and who can act. Check-in warns "You will be disqualified if you do not check in. Anyone on the team can check in," and a team whose players didn't all register in time sees why: "Your team is not eligible to participate because not all players registered in time."

Apex Legends Global Series: the full state map for a team, from registration open to games in progress, including the disqualified state.

Apex Legends Global Series: setting a lineup, then the Compete tab while teammates are still missing.

Tools for the People Running the League

Someone also has to confirm who is cleared to play. In the College League of Legends admin, the members table shows each student's release form status (approved, pending approval, or waiting for resubmission) next to their team, and a Pending Approval tab with a count shows what is still waiting. Permissions sit in their own column, apart from team role, so a player can also manage the roster.

College League of Legends admin: the members table with release form status, permissions and role. Red notes on the mockup suggest moving row actions into a menu and trying rows that open in edit state.

06

Four Weeks to One

4x Faster Delivery

Client portal delivery went from four weeks to one.

Top Revenue Source

Portals became the platform's most important source of revenue.

Profitable Company Milestone

Portals contributed to Battlefy reaching profitability for the first time.

How We Knew Portals Were Better for Players

Battlefy made decisions on data, but nothing measured how players experienced portals. I mapped the player journey by what each step should make people feel (aware, included, committed, welcomed, motivated) and used it to decide where to ask for feedback. I proposed integrating Qualtrics through its API so feedback could sit where it wouldn't interrupt play: always available, at the end of a flow, or in context. I had used the same approach on player ratings, where feedback participation rose from 0.5% to 16%.

Once that feedback came in, it showed players had a better experience on branded portals than on standard tournament pages. That validated Portals as a core product, not a services add-on.

A Design Function That Scaled

Portals was the first test of whether design at Battlefy could scale, and it showed that design could drive impact across the company. The process, the tiers and Comet became part of how Battlefy worked. I kept co-building and maintaining Comet, and coached Battlefy's product designers through branded experiences for brands including Nintendo, Sony, EA and the NFL.
