Designing for Lawyers

Clio

Clio builds the software legal professionals use to run their practices. For a lawyer, the product isn't a nice-to-have — it's where their clients, their matters, and their contracts live. That raises the bar for design: a confusing status or a missed date isn't just friction, it's risk.

I joined Clio in July 2026 as a Senior Product Designer, working across three areas that this page walks through: contract lifecycle management, AI suggestions, and Clio's native desktop apps.

My Role

Leading design vision. I lead the design vision for Clio's newest native desktop apps for Windows and macOS, and design end-to-end flows for contract lifecycle management.

Validating with lawyers. I've run dozens of rapid validation sessions with lawyers across multiple practice areas, testing both individual features and larger end-to-end flows.

Designing and building. I work as a hands-on designer-builder. I've pushed 26+ product updates myself, sharpening interaction details and visual polish across the app.

Raising the bar for the team. I've become the org reference for teaching designers to code and prototype with AI, coaching new design hires to deploy their own prototypes through GitHub and Clio's internal CI process.

How I work

Lawyers react to how something behaves, not to a picture of it. Testing high-fidelity coded prototypes lets me validate a single interaction and a full end-to-end flow in the same session — with the people who'll actually use it.

Clio Comments

As more of our prototypes started being generated with AI, reviews needed to catch up. I created Clio Comments, an internal tool that adds a commenting layer on top of AI-generated coded prototypes deployed internally. Instead of reviewing screenshots in one place and working software in another, feedback happens directly on the working prototype.

It's a small tool with a bigger purpose: helping the design function adapt to AI-native ways of working without losing the critique and collaboration that make design good.

02

Contract Lifecycle Management

The Problem

A contract doesn't end when it's signed. It moves through drafting, review, negotiation, approval, signature, and then a long life of obligations, deadlines, and renewals. Every stage has its own people, its own documents, and its own risks.

For lawyers, the hard part isn't any single step — it's knowing where every contract stands and what needs to happen next, without losing the details that matter. When that information is scattered across emails, documents, and memory, things slip.

Many Practice Areas, One Product

Lawyers don't all work the same way. Each practice area brings its own types of contracts, its own vocabulary, and its own definition of "done." The design challenge is building flows that are specific enough to feel right for each lawyer, but consistent enough to stay one product.

That's why I tested with lawyers across multiple practice areas, not just one. A flow that works beautifully for one group can quietly break for another — and you only find out by putting it in front of both.

Design principle

In legal work, clarity beats cleverness. Every status, date, and next step should be obvious at a glance — because a misread contract has real consequences.

Validating with Lawyers

I ran dozens of rapid validation sessions with lawyers using high-fidelity coded prototypes. Some sessions zoomed in on individual features; others walked through larger end-to-end flows, following a contract from one stage to the next.

Working in code changed the quality of the feedback. Lawyers could click, type, and move through a flow the way they would in their day — so instead of reacting to how a screen looks, they reacted to how the work actually felt. That made it much faster to separate what was confusing from what was simply unfamiliar.

The rapid cadence mattered too. Short cycles between sessions meant feedback from one lawyer could shape the prototype the next lawyer saw, so each round started from a better place.

03

AI Suggestions

The Problem

AI can save lawyers real time, but lawyers are accountable for every word that leaves their desk. A suggestion they can't trust isn't a shortcut — it's one more thing to double-check.

So the design problem isn't "how do we show AI output." It's how do we make AI suggestions easy to understand, easy to verify, and easy to accept or ignore — so they fit into the way lawyers already work instead of competing with it.

What Good Looks Like

Lawyers stay in control

Suggestions are offered, never applied silently. The lawyer decides what to accept, edit, or dismiss — and it's always clear which is which.

Easy to verify

A suggestion should make it obvious what it's based on, so reviewing it takes seconds, not a second investigation.

Part of the flow

Suggestions should show up where the work is already happening, at the moment they're useful — not in a separate place lawyers have to remember to check.

Why coded prototypes

AI doesn't behave the same way twice. A static mockup shows one perfect answer; a coded prototype shows the long, the short, and the slightly wrong ones. Designing for AI means designing for that variety — and testing it with real people.

Prototyping AI with AI

This is where the way I work and the thing I'm designing meet. I use AI-assisted prototyping to build working versions of AI features quickly, put them in front of lawyers, and see how they respond to real, imperfect output.

It's also why I built Clio Comments and why I coach other designers to code and deploy their own prototypes: when the product is powered by AI, the design process has to keep up with it.

04

Native Desktop Apps

The Problem

Clio's power users spend their whole day in the product. In a browser, Clio is one tab among dozens — competing with email, documents, and everything else on screen. A native app can do things a browser tab can't, and for people who live in the product all day, those details add up.

I led the design vision for Clio's newest native desktop apps for Windows and macOS: extending the web experience with native capabilities that supercharge power users.

Two Platforms, Two Sets of Rules

A desktop app that looks the same everywhere ends up feeling at home nowhere. Windows and macOS users have different expectations for how windows, menus, shortcuts, and controls behave — so the apps closely follow each platform's human interface guidelines.

The goal was for Clio to feel like it belongs on each operating system, while still being unmistakably Clio: one product, with platform-native behaviour where it counts.

Design principle

Native isn't a skin. It's respecting the habits people already have on their computer — so the app feels familiar on day one.

Designer-Builder

I didn't stop at the design file. As a hands-on designer-builder, I pushed 26+ product updates myself, sharpening interaction details and visual polish for a more refined overall experience.

Being able to ship small refinements directly means the gap between "this could be better" and "this is better" is hours, not sprints. It's also how the details that make an app feel native — spacing, states, transitions — actually make it into production.
