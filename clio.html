Clarity Where Mistakes Are Costly

The Context

Clio builds the software legal professionals use to run their practices — where their clients, matters, and contracts live. In this world, a confusing status or a missed step isn't friction; it's risk. Clarity is the most valuable thing design can deliver here.

This page covers three areas I work on: contract lifecycle management, AI suggestions, and Clio's native desktop apps. They look different, but each one is about the same thing — taking rules and workflows that are genuinely complex and making them simple to understand and act on.

What I Bring to the Team

Complex requirements, simple flows. I turn workflows with many people, versions, and rules into flows where the current state and the next step are always obvious.

Confidence before anything ships. Real users test working prototypes, so product decisions rest on evidence — and get made in weeks instead of months.

Designs that scale through the system. Prototypes are built from Clio's own Storybook components, so what users validate is what engineering can ship, and patterns stay consistent across teams.

Craft that reaches production. As a hands-on designer-builder, I've shipped 26+ refinements myself, so interaction details and visual polish don't get lost in handoff.

A better way of working for the whole team. I've become the design org's reference for AI-native work, coaching designers to build and deploy their own prototypes through GitHub and Clio's internal CI.

Clio Comments

As more prototypes were generated with AI, reviews had to catch up. I created Clio Comments, an internal tool that adds a commenting layer to coded prototypes deployed internally. Feedback now lands on working software instead of screenshots — shorter review cycles, and a design team that adopts new tools without losing the critique that keeps quality high.

02

Contract Lifecycle Management

The Problem

A contract passes through many hands before it's done: a paralegal drafts it, a senior lawyer reviews it, the firm approves it, the other side negotiates it, and several parties sign it. Every law practice runs this workflow a little differently.

When people lose track of where a contract is, who's working on it, or which version is current, the cost is real — duplicated effort, stalled negotiations, and documents going out that shouldn't. The product needed to make a long, multi-party process feel easy to follow.

A Lifecycle, Not a File

The key shift was treating the contract as something with a lifecycle — clear phases, clear owners, and clear transitions — instead of a document that gets passed around:

  • Drafting and internal review: a paralegal and a senior lawyer iterate through versions until the firm approves.
  • Negotiation: versions travel back and forth with external parties, each round redlined.
  • Finalization: the agreed version is cleaned up, compiled, and exported as a final PDF.
  • Signature: the final document goes out to anywhere from one to many signers.
  • Verification: the signed copy comes back and is checked against what was sent.

What It Unlocks for Lawyers

Everyone knows whose turn it is. Making it clear who holds the pen — inside the firm or on the other side — removes the waiting and duplicate edits that slow negotiations down.

The latest version is never in doubt. Clear versions, side-by-side comparison, and redlines mean no one edits or sends an outdated file.

Approvals stay visible. Paralegals and senior lawyers can see at a glance what's still a draft, what's waiting for approval, and what's ready to go out.

Signatures you can trust. Validating that the signed document matches the one that was sent protects the firm from changes slipping in between sending and signing.

The next step is always obvious. At every phase, the product answers "what can I do now?" before the user has to ask.

Design principle

At every step, a lawyer should be able to answer three questions at a glance: Where is this contract? Is this the latest version? What can I do next?

Built with the People Who Use It

We worked closely with a group of internal counsel at Clio who represent many different practice areas. They acted as our first users — some practice law, others serve as internal consultants — and we also talk to customers on a daily and weekly basis.

That range matters: a flow that works beautifully for one practice area can quietly break for another. Testing across practice areas means the design holds up for the whole customer base, not just the loudest group.

Weeks, Not a Year

Validation ran on 30-minute to one-hour sessions built around high-fidelity prototypes made with Claude — close enough to reality that lawyers could click and navigate as if it were the real product. Some sessions focused on a single feature; others followed a contract end to end.

Working prototypes close the loop on feedback faster, and the feedback is about the actual experience, not a picture of it. Because every prototype is built from Clio's Storybook components — enforced through a set of Claude skills — what lawyers validate is what we can ship. After each round, we compiled the feedback and iterated, so every session started from a better version than the last.

The impact on speed

What would usually take the company a year is now taking weeks. Fast, real-user validation moves the team from idea to end result quickly — with contract lifecycle management on track for a pilot release within the next month.

03

AI Suggestions

The Problem

AI can save lawyers real time, but lawyers are accountable for every word that leaves their desk. A suggestion they can't trust isn't a shortcut — it's one more thing to double-check.

The value of AI here depends entirely on trust: suggestions have to be easy to understand, easy to verify, and easy to accept or ignore, so they fit the way lawyers already work instead of competing with it.

What Good Looks Like

People stay in control

Suggestions are offered, never applied silently. The user decides what to accept, edit, or dismiss — and it's always clear which is which.

Easy to verify

A suggestion makes it obvious what it's based on, so reviewing it takes seconds, not a second investigation.

Part of the flow

Suggestions show up where the work is already happening, at the moment they're useful — not in a separate place people have to remember to check.

Designing for How AI Really Behaves

AI doesn't behave the same way twice. A static mockup shows one perfect answer; a working prototype shows the long, the short, and the slightly wrong ones. Building AI features as working prototypes and testing them with real users lets us design for that variety — including the moments when the AI gets it wrong.

Human judgment

AI can draft the words and even generate the interface. Decisions about trust, copy, and accessibility still need human judgment — that's where design earns its place.

04

Native Desktop Apps

The Problem

Clio's power users spend their whole day in the product. In a browser, Clio is one tab among dozens — competing with email, documents, and everything else on screen. For people who live in the product, that friction adds up.

I led the design vision for Clio's newest native desktop apps for Windows and macOS, extending the web experience with native capabilities that make power users faster.

An App That Feels at Home

Windows and macOS users have different expectations for how windows, menus, shortcuts, and controls behave. Closely following each platform's human interface guidelines means people's existing habits just work — the app feels familiar on day one, while still being unmistakably Clio.

Respecting a platform's conventions is also what makes a product feel trustworthy. When things behave the way people expect, they spend their attention on their work, not on learning the tool.

Design principle

Native isn't a skin. It's respecting the habits people already have on their device — so the product feels familiar from the first click.

Polish That Ships

As a hands-on designer-builder, I pushed 26+ product updates myself, sharpening interaction details and visual polish across the app. The gap between "this could be better" and "this is better" shrinks from sprints to hours — and the small details that make an app feel native actually make it to production.
