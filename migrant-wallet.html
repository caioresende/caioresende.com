UX Case Study

Migrant Wallet

People who live between countries keep money in two or three currencies, and the apps built to move it don't show what they have in total. I designed and built Migrant Wallet around one question: how much am I worth right now? The shipped app answers it in a two-second glance, 60% less time than the early builds needed.

Sole design engineer · Sketches to App Store in 3 months Multi-currency personal finance for newcomers Flutter · Firebase · iOS, iPad, Android

01

One Question, Five Seconds to Answer

What I owned

Everything, from competitive analysis and product specs to information architecture, the Flutter front end, the Firebase back end and the App Store launch. With no handoffs, what I designed is what shipped.

Knowing what you have in total means converting each balance one at a time and adding it up in your head. How much are my Canadian savings worth in reais? What's my net worth across three countries? I face these questions myself, which is why this problem mattered to me.

Apps like Wise, Remitly and Western Union are built to move money. None of them add up what you hold across banks and countries into one total. That became the product's promise: "Money talks, we translate it."

Time to Financial Clarity

The one measure I judged the dashboard against: the seconds it takes to answer "how much am I worth right now?" across all your currencies. The build I started from needed about five seconds of focused reading. The target was a two-second glance.

That build answered with a spreadsheet: purple headers, monospace numbers, and a table with rows for "Debit (Assets)", "Credit (Liabilities)" and "Net Sum" in each currency. At one point the table needed a legend just to be read.

Early builds: favourite currencies with no total, then a debit/credit table, the same table with a legend, and finally in monospace. Every version made you read rows and do arithmetic.

Four problems in that build

I audited that build and found four problems. The first explains the five seconds. The other three are why the app now has a converter, rate timestamps and a picker that blocks duplicates.

Reading instead of seeing

Rows of same-weight numbers with no hierarchy. You had to find the right column, then the right row, before you had an answer.

Doing the math yourself

There was no converter. To know what 1,000 CAD was in BRL, you looked up the rate on another tab, remembered it, came back and multiplied. The app made people do the exact math it existed to remove.

No sign the rates were current

No timestamp and no refresh. When the numbers are your money, not knowing whether a rate is from today or last week creates doubt.

No guardrails

You could pick the same currency twice, with no warning and no validation.

02

From a Spreadsheet to a Glance

Split checking from setting up

The early builds had two tabs, Pockets and Currencies. Checking your money and configuring currencies shared space, and converting had no home at all. I reorganised the app by how often you do each thing: Wallets (daily), Instant Rates (often), Currencies (once, at setup) and Feedback (now and then). With 13 screens in total, a flat four-tab bar puts every main destination one tap away, with no menu to dig through.

I also renamed Pockets to Wallets. "Pockets" was creative, but "Wallets" is understood by someone reading English as a second or third language.

Eight typefaces tried on the same overview table, to find the one where long numbers stay easiest to scan.

Three totals, then the detail

The table became two zones. At the top, one bar per favourite currency shows your net total in that currency. Below, each wallet is a card with its own amount and its value in each of your currencies, as coloured pills. Instead of reading a grid, you read three numbers. Assets, liabilities and net still exist, one level down in a sheet, for when you need them.

Colour carries the rest. Each of your three currencies gets its own colour in the totals, the wallet pills and the converter, so you can tell them apart before reading the code.

November to December: converted values in tinted cells, then cards, then coloured pills, then stacked totals per currency. The full breakdown moved into a sheet, one level below the glance.

03

Type Once, See Every Direction

The second job is conversion: what is this amount in my other currencies? With three currencies there are six directions, and the early builds offered none of them. My first sketch set the rule the shipped screen follows, in a note at the bottom of the page: update in real time, with no waiting and no "update" button. The wireframe and the first coded screen still had one.

From paper I moved to a wireframe of all six pairs, then to studies of code position, alignment, unit rates (1 EUR = 6.34 BRL) versus bulk amounts (100,000 EUR = 634,718 BRL), and very long results. The shipped screen keeps what they settled: the code sits after the number as a pill, results right-align so the digits line up, and the matrix converts the amount you type instead of showing unit rates.

Paper sketches with my notes, a wireframe of all six pairs and the first coded screen, then studies of currency-code position, alignment and long amounts.

The six-line first version gave way to one card per pair, which no longer fit on one screen. The version that worked groups conversions by the currency you start from: three rows, each showing your amount in the other two currencies. You type once in the bar at the bottom, and all six directions update as you type, as the first sketch asked.

Below the matrix, a 1:1 converter with a swap button covers any other pair, including currencies outside your three.

Rates you can check

Every screen with converted numbers shows when rates were last downloaded, next to a refresh button. Rates are saved on the device, so the app keeps working on a plane or in the subway, and you decide when to refresh.

One card per pair, layout debugging in code, three grouped rows, and the shipped screen with timestamp, refresh and the 1:1 converter.

04

One Pill, Every Screen

The currency pill, a small colour-coded badge with the ISO code, is the app's most reused component. It appears on wallet cards, in the matrix, in the picker, in settings rows and in forms, so it had to hold up at every size. I tested shape, weight, border and fill, then built a colour set that keeps your chosen currencies distinct without fighting the sage and cream palette. Across the full currency list the hues repeat, one reason colour can't be the only signal (see What I'd Fix Next).

Pill variations across shape, weight, border and fill, and the colour set applied to the currency list and the matrix.

Because it's one component, currency selection works the same way in all three tabs where it appears: a dropdown with the pill inside, so you learn it once. The picker searches by name or code, and currencies you've already chosen are greyed out, which closes the duplicate-currency gap from the audit.

The wallet form opens as a bottom sheet, so the dashboard stays in view behind it. Debit or credit is a pair of radio buttons rather than a dropdown, so both options are visible before you choose. Delete sits alone in the top corner, in red, away from Save. At the top, a preview shows the amount in your other currencies as you type.

Before: plain dropdowns and a redundant "Current Selection" list. After: pills in the currency dropdowns, chosen currencies greyed out, a searchable picker, and an earlier build of the wallet form, with its live conversion preview but a currency field still without the pill.

05

Teach the Idea Before Asking for Data

A new user has no currencies and no wallets, so on day one the app's value is invisible. Onboarding had to teach one idea: everything you own, in any currency, adds up to one total in the currencies you think in.

Four screens, one idea each: what the app does ("Money talks, we translate it"), your three currencies ("How rich are you in…"), adding it all up ("Everything you own, one total") and converting ("Triple conversion, zero effort"). The last button reads "Set currencies" instead of "Done", so onboarding ends on the first real setup step.

The four onboarding screens. The last one hands off straight to currency setup.

The aggregation screen went through several passes: a serif headline over faint copy, then the app's own typeface with body copy that says where the total shows up, then a version that tagged every asset with its country and labelled the totals Currency #1, #2 and #3.

Three passes at "Everything you own, one total." The iOS version in the four-screen set above has shorter copy and no country tags.

Empty states that point to the next step

Every empty screen names what's missing and puts the fix right there: no currencies yet, "Set currencies"; no wallets yet, the add button. Before, people had to find the Currencies tab on their own.

Empty Wallets and Instant Rates screens, each with the next step in place.

06

Built for Long Numbers and Long Words

Building it myself meant I could test layouts with numbers nobody puts in a mockup: balances in the trillions, negative totals, high-denomination currencies. In debug builds Flutter paints a warning stripe where a row or column overflows, so most breaks showed up on screen as I tested.

One fix I ruled out was truncating with an ellipsis. A financial number with its last digits cut off shows the wrong amount. Amounts that don't fit now scroll horizontally on their own, so you can read every digit without tapping.

Stress tests in debug builds: overflow warnings on wallet rows, the card layout and the converter, then the ellipsis version I ruled out.

Three languages, one layout

In December I shipped Brazilian Portuguese and French, for corridors like Brazil to the US and Portugal, Senegal and Côte d'Ivoire to France, and Haiti to Canada. French was the hardest fit: "Taux Instantanés" is much longer than "Instant Rates", and it still fits the tab bar without truncation. A few screens still mix languages.

Portuguese builds: a fully translated tab bar, with the longer "Selecione moeda" label overflowing the dropdowns; currency settings still under an English tab bar, one of the mixed-language gaps; and the Add wallet form.

08

What I'd Fix Next

In priority order.

P0 · Critical

Very large numbers

Auto-scrolling covers most cases, but amounts past about ten digits still break some layouts, especially in high-denomination currencies like the yen or the dong. Next step: scale the type down first. Abbreviating to 1.5M or 2.3B drops digits just as an ellipsis does, so it's the last resort.

P1 · High

Colour can't be the only signal

About 8% of men have some form of colour vision deficiency, and the currency system leans on colour. The ISO codes are there, but small. I'd make them larger and add a second cue, such as shape or pattern.

P1 · High

The three-currency ceiling

The app caps you at three currencies without saying why, and some lives don't fit in three: a Brazilian in Canada with US investments and family in Portugal needs four. I'd raise the limit to five with a scrollable summary, or at least explain the cap.

P2 · Medium

Form validation

Wallet forms accept empty or non-numeric amounts without an error, and Save looks active whether the form is complete or not.

P2 · Medium

A real tablet layout

On tablets, it's still the phone layout with more spacing. It needs a sidebar and a split view, with wallets and conversions side by side.

Tablet today: the phone layout, stretched.

What I'd keep from this project: pick the one question people open the app to answer, time how long it takes, and judge the main screen against that number.
